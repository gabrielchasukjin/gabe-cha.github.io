Fine-tuning Nemotron-30B to crack six kinds of reasoning puzzles

NVIDIA Nemotron Model Reasoning Challenge (Kaggle) · gsc2133@columbia.edu
Gabriel Cha
Kaggle leaderboard
● Live — competition in progress

The NVIDIA Nemotron Model Reasoning Challenge asks one deceptively simple question: can you fine-tune an open Nemotron model to reason through puzzles it has never seen? My entry is a study in something I keep coming back to — that for deterministic tasks, the quality of your chain-of-thought data matters more than almost anything you do to the model.

The challenge

The benchmark is six families of puzzles, three easy and three genuinely hard:

  • Numeral conversion, unit conversion, gravity/physics — mechanical once you know the rule.
  • Substitution cipher — recover plaintext from a scrambled alphabet.
  • Bit manipulation — infer a hidden boolean function mapping input bits to output bits.
  • Equation transform — discover the structural rule rewriting one expression into another.

You submit a LoRA adapter (rank 32 max) on top of Nemotron-Nano-30B, a mixture-of-experts model. Scoring is exact-match on the boxed final answer, so there's nowhere to hide — the model either lands the answer or it doesn't.

My approach

1. Treat the chain-of-thought as the product

My first dataset let an LLM write the reasoning traces freehand. On the hard categories they were garbage — confident-sounding prose like "applying the compound operation…" that never actually did the math. The model dutifully learned to imitate the hand-waving. So I rebuilt the data around a rule: every reasoning step has to be verified, not vibed.

2. Solve the puzzles programmatically, then teach the trace

For the hard categories I wrote deterministic solvers and used their work as the chain-of-thought:

  • Cipher solver — character alignment plus a 77-word vocabulary gap-fill, solving 98.4% of cases.
  • Bit solver — per-bit boolean decomposition (out[0] = in[1] XNOR in[7]), solving 83.5%.
  • Equation transform — structural analysis with the vague tails stripped out.

I also dropped 265 bit puzzles I couldn't solve cleanly — better to teach nothing than to teach noise.

3. Structured beats "natural"

I tried rewriting every trace into a chatty, DeepSeek-R1-style voice. It scored worse. For puzzles with one correct answer, a rigid, structured format teaches the pattern more reliably than fluent prose. That result reshaped how I think about CoT data.

4. SFT to teach format, GRPO to teach reasoning

The plan that moved the needle is a two-stage pipeline — the same shape NVIDIA's own AIMO-2 winning team used:

  • SFT teaches the output format: <think>…</think> then \boxed{answer}.
  • GRPO (reinforcement learning) teaches the model to actually be right. Because every puzzle has a verifiable answer, the reward signal is exact — no reward model needed, and I can synthesize unlimited training puzzles.

The leaderboard

A solo entry at 0.83, 0.07 off the top of a 4,301-team field, with a day still on the clock and the final 50% of the test set yet to be scored. The score climbed across the run — 0.61 → 0.70 → 0.74 → 0.83 — every jump traceable to a specific change in the data, not the hyperparameters.

What I took away

  • Data quality is the lever. Verified, programmatic chain-of-thought beat both freehand LLM traces and a frontier model's natural-language rewrites.
  • For deterministic tasks, structure > fluency. The "nicer-sounding" dataset lost.
  • Verifiable answers are a gift. They turn RL from a research project into a clean reward function — which is exactly why the SFT→GRPO pipeline is the path to closing that last 0.07.
